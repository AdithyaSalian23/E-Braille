Why this exists

"The braille literacy rate in India is just 1%, against a general literacy rate of 77.7%."

Of the world's 37 million blind people, an estimated 90% live in developing countries — around 15 million of them in India, the highest number of any country. Commercial e-Braille displays cost roughly $2,000 in the US and ₹70,000–1,00,000 in India, putting them out of reach for most of the people who need them.

E-Braille was built to close that gap: a compact, self-contained Raspberry Pi device that teaches Grade 1 Braille through audio-guided, gamified practice — no internet connection, no instructor, and no assumption that the learner already knows where to start.

It runs three learning modes — Practice Alphabets, Practice Numbers, and a Duolingo-style Quiz — and reads every prompt aloud, so the whole experience can be used independently from power-on to mastery.

How it teaches

Three modes, one Braille cell.

Every mode drives the same six-solenoid cell — what changes is the pacing and the pressure. Beginners get untimed repetition; confident learners get tested.

Mode 01

Practice Alphabets

Cycles through A–Z. The device raises each letter's pattern, speaks "Type letter A," and waits — no timer — while the learner feels the cell and types it back on the Braille keypad before moving on.

Mode 02

Practice Numbers

The same untimed loop, run across 0–9, reinforcing the numeric patterns learners will need for dates, addresses, and everyday reading.

Mode 03

Quiz Mode

Five random alphanumeric characters, three attempts each. Get it wrong twice and the device raises the correct pattern for you to feel and learn, then asks again — like Duolingo, but built for fingertips.

Under the hood

From a button press to a raised dot.

A Raspberry Pi 3B reads the keypad, drives an 8-channel relay board, and the relays switch enough current to fire six solenoid actuators — the electromechanical "fingers" that physically form each Braille pattern.

01

Button press

One of nine push buttons signals a mode change, an Enter, or a Next — read on Raspberry Pi GPIO pins 19, 21 and 23.

02

Audio prompt

The Pi speaks the next instruction through the speaker — "Type letter A," "Correct, press next," or the mode's opening cue.

03

activateBrailleSolenoids()

The controller looks up the dot pattern for the target character and fires the matching solenoids through the relay board, since GPIO pins alone can't supply the current a solenoid draws.

04

Read & respond

The learner feels the raised dots and types the combination back on the Braille keypad.

05

checkLetterTyped()

Input is compared against the expected pattern; a match triggers "Correct, press next" and advances the sequence.
